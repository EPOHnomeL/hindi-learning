Hindi grammar & core words

The canonical language for this workspace. Grammar terms follow Snell & Weightman's Teach Yourself Hindi; vocabulary is anchored in the BSI OV Bible. A term is added only once it can be used correctly.

Grammar terms

Present habitual (imperfective present)

A verb form describing a routine, a general truth, or a repeated/timeless action — “walks”, “lives”, “does not smoke”. Built from the verb stem + -ता / -ती / -ते, normally followed by the auxiliary है / हैं. Example: चलना “to walk” → वह चलता है “he walks”. (Handbook pp. 74–77.)

Avoid: “present tense”, “present simple” — too vague; Hindi has a separate present continuous.

Verb stem

What is left of a verb after removing the infinitive ending -ना. चलना → चल, बैठना → बैठ, होना → हो. All tense endings attach to the stem.

Avoid: “root” — reserve that for later morphology discussion.

Postposition

A relationship word (in, on, to, from, of) that in Hindi comes after the noun, not before it — मकान में “in the house”. The five simple ones: में (in), पर (on), को (to), से (by/with/from), तक (up to). (Handbook §3.1, p.40.)

Avoid: “preposition” — that is the English order; Hindi reverses it.

Direct case / Oblique case

A noun has two cases. The direct case is its plain form, used for the subject of a verb. The oblique is the form it must take when a postposition follows it. Masculine -ा nouns shift -ा→-े in the oblique singular (कमरा→कमरे); nouns of all classes take -ओं in the oblique plural (कमरों, दुष्टों, पापियों). (Handbook §3.2, pp.41–42.)

Avoid: calling everything “the plural” — -ओं signals plural and oblique.

का / के / की — the genitive “of”

The postposition meaning “of / ’s”. Like all postpositions it puts the preceding noun in the oblique; it also agrees with the following noun — का (masc. sg.), के (masc. oblique/plural), की (feminine). दुष्टों की युक्ति “the counsel of the wicked”.

Avoid: treating का/के/की as three different words — they are one word agreeing.

Relative pronoun (जो)

The word “(the one) who / which”, used to attach a describing clause to a noun. It is both singular and plural. All Hindi relatives begin with ज- (जो who, जब when, जहाँ where), whereas the matching question words begin with क- (कौन, कब, कहाँ). (Handbook §13.1 p.162, §13.4 p.168.)

Avoid: confusing जो (relative “who”) with कौन (interrogative “who?”).

Correlative (वह … जो)

The “answering” word that pairs with a relative. Hindi keeps a balanced pair of clauses where English fuses them: वह पुरुष जो … = “the man who …” (lit. “that man, who …”). The relative clause (ज-) is matched by a correlative (वह/वे, तब, वहाँ). (Handbook §13.1, p.162.)

Avoid: expecting one fused word like English “who”; look for the ज-/वह pair.

Auxiliary (here, the है-auxiliary)

The “to be” verb (हूँ / है / हैं / हो) tagged onto a participle to anchor it in present time. In elevated/literary and biblical Hindi it is often dropped, especially in lists and timeless statements — which is why Psalm 1:1 reads चलता … होता … बैठता है (auxiliary only on the last verb).

Avoid: “helper verb” — ambiguous; Hindi also has true compound-verb helpers.

Vocabulary · BSI OV, Psalm 1

धन्य
dhanya — blessed, fortunate, happy. The opening word of Psalm 1’s blessing — क्या ही धन्य “how blessed!”.
पुरुष
puruṣ — man, person. Masculine noun. (Printed in the OV as पुरूष.)
जो
jo — who / which, the relative pronoun that opens a relative clause (“the man who walks…”). Pairs later with वह “that (one)”.
चलना
calnā — to walk, to move, to go along. Habitual: चलता है “walks”.
बैठना
baiṭhnā — to sit. Habitual: बैठता है “sits”.
होना
honā — to be / to become. The most important verb in Hindi; also the source of the है-auxiliary. खड़ा होना “to stand” (lit. “to become standing”).
नहीं
nahīṁ — not, the standard negator, placed before the verb. न (na) is its shorter, more literary twin, used for “and not / nor”.
